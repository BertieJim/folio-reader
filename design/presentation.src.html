A reader that gets out of the way.

You asked for a material reader: fresh and light to look at, easy to fill with your own files, and immersive the moment you start reading. Step 1Discover

What you asked for, and what I heard

A brief is a starting point. Before drawing anything I restated it, listed the assumptions I would have to make, and separated what I could decide from what only you can.

Your brief
  1. Fresh, light, great aesthetics, proper colour themes.
  2. Create folders; download or transfer material into them — TXT, MOBI, DOCX and so on.
  3. Once reading begins, it should be immersive.

Plus, from our conversation: an AI assistant will be added later, so the app has to be built to accept one.

What I heard
  • “Material”, not “books”. Novels, yes — but also course handouts, reports, handbooks. Covers won’t exist; the app must make its own.
  • Files the person already owns. No store, no feed, no account needed to start. Import is the front door, so it must be effortless.
  • Immersive means disappearing. Not animations — the absence of chrome, and controls that appear only when asked for.
  • Fresh and light rules out the two clichés: cream-and-serif “library” and black-and-neon “tech”.

Step 2Research

Who this is for, and what already exists

This is desk research: three personas drawn from the brief and from how reading apps are actually used, and a scan of the apps people would compare us with. I recommend five short user interviews before we commit engineering time — see Decide.

Persona

The collector

Has years of novels in TXT and MOBI from forums and an old Kindle, in both Chinese and English. Reads an hour a night.

Pain: half the apps mangle GBK text or choke on MOBI; folders are an afterthought.

Needs from us: robust formats, eye-care themes, a shelf that stays tidy at 300 books.

Persona

The student

Gets handouts and readings as DOCX and PDF through a portal. Reads on the commute, one course at a time.

Pain: moving files from laptop to phone is a chore; nothing remembers where she stopped.

Needs from us: Wi-Fi transfer of a whole folder, a folder per course, progress that survives.

Persona

The professional

Receives reports and handbooks as links in chat. Reads in fragments between meetings.

Pain: opening a document is easy; finding it again next week is not.

Needs from us: import from a link or the share sheet, “continue reading” front and centre, later an assistant to summarise.

Three takeaways that shaped the design.

1. Import friction is where people give up — every app above makes “get my file in” harder than “read”. 2. Immersion is a discipline, not a feature: Kindle wins by showing less. 3. Folders exist everywhere and are hidden everywhere; putting them on the home screen is a differentiator on its own.

Step 3Define

Principles, structure and the three flows that matter

Before any pixels: three principles to judge every screen against, a map of the app, and the flows the prototype had to get right.

One gesture in

Any file, from anywhere, lands in the library with a title, a cover and chapters — with no settings to visit first. Import is the front door, so it is the big round button.

Tidy without tending

Folders are a name and a colour. The shelf sorts itself by what you touched last; unfiled books are never lost; the home screen opens on where you left off.

Disappear when reading

The page is edge to edge. Two faint lines of status in the text’s own ink. Controls arrive on a centre tap and leave on the next.

Step 4Design

Visual direction

Fresh and light, with enough character to be recognised. Colour does the “fresh”; typography does the “character”; whitespace does the “light”.

Colour

One accent — a calm verdigris — on a green-biased off-white. The off-white is what makes the app feel fresh rather than cream-and-bookish; the single accent keeps every screen quiet so that book covers, which are generated in eight soft tints, are the colourful thing.

Cover tints. Files rarely come with artwork, so every book gets a typographic cover on one of eight tints, chosen from the title so the same book always gets the same colour. Folders use the same set.

Reading themes. The reader has its own palette, separate from the app’s, because a page is a different object from a screen. Four themes cover daylight, evening, long sessions and bed; each keeps its status text in the same ink as the body at half strength so nothing competes with the words.

How it was tested

Because there is no converter on this machine, I generated one real file per format — a UTF-8 text with “Chapter n” headings, a GBK-encoded Chinese text with “第n章” headings, a Word document with two heading levels, a four-chapter EPUB with metadata, and a PalmDOC-compressed MOBI with author and title records — and imported all five through the actual file picker in a scripted browser session.

Step 6Decide

What I need from you

Six decisions, each with my recommendation. Reply with a number and a word and I can move.

  • 1 · Name and brand.“Folio” is a placeholder. Recommendation: keep it through the prototype phase; decide the real name after user interviews, when we know how people describe the app.
  • 2 · Platform after H5.A native shell (iOS/Android, or a wrapped web view) unlocks Wi-Fi transfer, share-sheet import and true full screen. Recommendation: ship this H5 as the internal test build now; go native for release, reusing the screens as they are.
  • 3 · Folders: flat or nested?Nesting adds power and a lot of navigation. Recommendation: flat folders in v1; measure how often people ask for sub-folders.
  • 4 · Accounts and sync.Everything works on-device today. Recommendation: no account in v1. Add optional sync later — it also becomes the channel for the assistant if it ever needs a server.
  • 5 · PDF priority.If your materials are mostly PDF, it moves to the front of the queue and needs its own reading surface. Recommendation: tell me the real mix of formats in your library; I will scope PDF from that.
  • 6 · The AI assistant’s entry points.The prototype already normalises every format into one shape — chapters made of paragraphs — which is exactly what an assistant needs. Recommendation: three entry points, in this order: select text → “Ask”, a chapter-level “Summarise / Explain” in the Contents panel, and a library-level “Find where I read about…”. Say which of these matters most and I will design that surface next.
One request that is not a decision.

Five thirty-minute conversations with people who match the personas — ideally two collectors, two students, one professional — before we commit engineering time. I will run them and bring back what changes.
